Sep 2022 – Mar 2023  ·  High School Astronomy Research

Dobsonian
Telescope,
From Scratch

1st Place · Bucks County Science Research Competition

Why build a telescope when you can just buy one?

Because building one teaches you things no store-bought version ever could, like the exact moment a PVC sewer pipe sourced from a junkyard turns out not to be perfectly circular, and the whole optical path depends on it being circular.

This project started with a question: could a cost-effective Dobsonian reflector be built from scratch using repurposed materials, then used for real science with results worth comparing to NASA’s data? The answer turned out to be yes: 98.88% accuracy to NASA’s published orbital period of Io, one of Jupiter’s moons.

A Dobsonian is a reflector telescope that bounces light off a large primary mirror at the bottom of the tube up to a small secondary mirror, which redirects it sideways into an eyepiece. The design is popular because it lets you build something large and powerful cheaply, which made it the right design to attempt at home, in high school, with junkyard parts.

The PVC problem. The main tube came from a junkyard, a large-diameter sewer pipe that wasn’t quite circular. The fix involved precisely shimming the spider mount with washers, iterating on the alignment, and verifying collimation each time. Every adjustment had to be exact.
The science. Once built and collimated, the telescope was used to observe Io over multiple nights. Comparing the measured period to NASA’s published value: 98.88% accuracy.

The Kitchen Table Phase
Planning & Calculations

Every build starts somewhere: a sheet of plywood, a TI-30X, a box cutter, a ruler, and ambitious notes. Before cutting anything, focal length, tube diameter, mirror size, and mounting geometry all had to be calculated.

02
The Main Tube & First Assembly
Primary Optical Tube / Junkyard PVC

The main tube holds the primary mirror at the bottom and the secondary mirror assembly at the top. This one came from a junkyard: a large-diameter PVC sewer pipe. The right photo shows first full assembly to verify the calculations held up.

The PVC wasn’t perfectly round. Precise shimming and careful iteration fixed it, but everything downstream depended on getting this exactly right.
03
The Rocker Box & Dobsonian Mount
Alt-Az Mount / Plywood Build

The rocker box lets the telescope tip up and down (altitude), while the circular ground board allows it to rotate left and right (azimuth). Left shows prototype; right shows the finished telescope at dusk, painted and ready.

04
Painting & Light Control
Matte Black Interior / Light Baffle

The tube was spray-painted matte black inside and out. Stray light creates glare that washes out faint objects, so the interior had to be dark enough to keep the optical path clean before the final hardware went on.

05
Focuser & Finder Scope
SVBONY Rack & Pinion · Astromania 6×30

Left: the focuser slides the eyepiece in and out until the image sharpens. Had to be positioned so the eyepiece lands exactly at the primary mirror’s focal plane. Right: the finished telescope with the finder scope mounted up top as a wide-field navigation aid for locating targets.

06
The Spider
Secondary Mirror Holder / Three-Vane Assembly

The spider is the three-vane metal assembly that holds the secondary mirror centered inside the tube. The secondary redirects the light cone rising from the primary mirror 90° out through the focuser to the eyepiece.

The non-circular tube caused the most trouble here. Shimming the primary mirror cell with washers to get the alignment right, then recollimating from scratch, was one of the more painstaking parts of the build.
07
The Primary Mirror & Cell
8” Parabolic Mirror / Three-Point Support

The primary mirror is a precision-ground 8-inch parabolic reflector. The three thumb-screws are collimation adjusters. The right shows the mirror cell from behind: a plywood triangle with three spring-loaded clips so the mirror can flex with temperature changes without being stressed.

08
Looking Down the Barrel
The Collimation View

Looking straight down: the primary mirror at the bottom, framed by the tube’s interior. When everything’s aligned, the secondary mirror’s reflection appears centered in the primary, and the eye appears centered in the secondary. Three concentric circles. Early sessions ran close to an hour; eventually it was under ten minutes.

2025 · Robotics · Embedded Systems

Gesture-Controlled
Robotic Arm

A robotic arm that does what your hand does.

This is a six-axis robotic arm system that follows hand gestures end-to-end: a 3D-printed arm with a wearable glove transmitter. Put on the glove, move your hand, and the arm mirrors it in real time over Bluetooth.

The arm covers gripper, wrist (two axes), elbow, shoulder pair, and base rotation. Every joint is independently controlled, with smooth trajectories and hardware protection limits built into the firmware. The whole thing runs on two separate power rails tied to a common ground to isolate the high-current stepper from the servo logic.

The hardest part wasn't any single component. It was getting everything to talk to each other reliably: flex sensors, dual IMUs, two Arduinos, a Bluetooth link, a PWM driver, and a stepper driver, all in the same pipeline without dropping commands or glitching a servo.

The glove. The gauntlet holds an Arduino Nano reading three flex sensors and dual MPU6050 IMUs. Finger curl maps to gripper position; wrist tilt maps to wrist joints; arm orientation maps to shoulder and elbow. Gestures are encoded into single-character commands (F/f, U/D, L/R) and sent over HC-05 Bluetooth to the arm.
The arm. An Arduino Uno receives commands and maps them to joint setpoints using incremental updates. A PCA9685 PWM board drives the MG996-class servos, while a NEMA-17 stepper on an A4988 driver handles base rotation for higher torque. Two battery packs: 6V NiMH for the servos, 11.1V LiPo for the stepper.

Build Log

Every part, explained.

01
The Arm
3D-Printed / Six-Axis / NEMA-17 Base

Fully 3D-printed across six axes: gripper, two wrist joints, elbow, shoulder pair, and a rotating base. The left photo shows one of the first major printed assemblies: the base bearing, gear, and fastener stack that everything else had to build on. The base uses a NEMA-17 stepper rather than a servo for significantly more torque. MG996-class servos handle the upper joints, driven through a PCA9685 PWM board over I2C so the Arduino Uno isn't tied up generating PWM signals directly.

02
The Gripper
MG996 Servo / Printed Gear Clamp

The gripper uses a printed gear clamp mechanism driven by an MG996-class servo. The two jaw pieces mesh against each other so both sides move symmetrically when the servo turns. Getting the gear mesh right took several iterations: too tight and the servo stalls under grip load; too loose and there's backlash in the clamp motion. Tuned by adjusting print tolerances until it closed cleanly without slipping.

03
Electronics & Power
Arduino Uno / PCA9685 / Dual Power Rails

Two power rails tied to a common ground: a 6V NiMH pack for the PCA9685 and servos, and a separate 11.1V LiPo for the NEMA-17 stepper via an A4988 driver. Isolating the stepper prevents its high current draw from spiking the servo supply. The Arduino Uno receives Bluetooth commands and maps them to joint setpoints, with per-channel firmware limits to protect both the hardware and the printed structure.

04
The Gauntlet
Arduino Nano / Flex Sensors / Dual MPU6050

Three flex sensors detect finger curl; two MPU6050 IMUs track wrist tilt and arm orientation. The close-up shows the glove-side electronics packed onto the wearable board: Arduino Nano, sensors, wiring, and battery all moving with the hand. The Nano reads all five inputs simultaneously and encodes the gesture into single-character commands: F/f for finger open/close, U/D for up/down, L/R for rotation. Compact by design, keeping the Bluetooth link fast and the arm-side parsing simple.

05
The Bluetooth Link
HC-05 Master/Slave / Serial Command Protocol

Paired HC-05 modules: master on the glove, slave on the arm. Once connected, the link behaves like a serial cable. One byte per command, no framing overhead. The Uno maps each character to a joint setpoint using incremental updates rather than absolute positions, so the arm moves smoothly even if commands arrive at variable intervals.

2026 · ECE 2564 · Embedded Systems

MSP432 LaunchPad
+ BoosterPack

A semester of making tiny hardware do very specific things.

This sequence was completed for my Embedded Systems class using the MSP432 LaunchPad and BoosterPack. The projects covered LCD graphics, UART, GPIO buttons, LEDs, timers, joystick input, ADC, PWM, interrupts, and low-level hardware control.

The through-line was learning how to structure embedded applications as finite state machines, keep the main loop responsive, and move hardware-specific work into a HAL so the application code stayed readable.

The hardware. MSP432 LaunchPad, Educational BoosterPack, Crystalfontz 128x128 LCD, joystick, pushbuttons, RGB LEDs, UART over USB, ADC14, Timer32, TimerA PWM, and DriverLib.
The pattern. Each build separated application state from hardware access, used named constants instead of magic numbers, and kept the UI responsive through non-blocking code or interrupt-driven peripherals.

Project 1 · FSM · UART · MSP432

Minesweeper
on the BoosterPack

LCD Game · Adjustable Board · Non-blocking

Minesweeper, but make it fit on a 128x128 screen.

This project turned the MSP432 and BoosterPack into a full Minesweeper game, complete with title, menu, instructions, setup, game, and results screens. The LCD shows the board, the terminal handles coordinate input, and the whole thing runs as a finite state machine with separate screen handlers.

Before each game, the player chooses the number of mines, rows, and columns over UART. The game supports selecting cells, flagging mines, revealing blank regions with recursive flood fill, switching baud rates with BB1, and showing the active baud rate through the BoosterPack RGB LED.

View code

The game loop. The app moves through title, menu, instructions, setup, game, and results screens, with each screen getting its own draw and handler functions.
The embedded part. UART input drives the board, BB1 cycles baud rate between 9600, 19200, 38400, and 57600 bps, and the RGB LED shows the active rate in real time.

Project 3 · Interrupts · ADC · PWM

Interrupt-Driven
Hardware Tasks

GPIO · Timer32 · UART · Joystick · Color Mixer

One board, five screens, many ways to wake the processor up.

This project was built around interrupts: GPIO button presses, Timer32, UART receive, ADC joystick sampling, and PWM color mixing. The application cycles through a main menu and five task screens, each one demonstrating a different peripheral behavior on the MSP432 and BoosterPack.

The hardest task was the Color Mixer. TimerA PWM drives the red, green, and blue LED channels independently through port mapping. JSB cycles the selected color, BB1 increases the value, BB2 decreases it, and the BoosterPack LED updates immediately.

View code

The interrupt stack. GPIO interrupts handled buttons, ADC14 continuously sampled the joystick axes, UART receive woke the processor from LPM0, and Timer32 used a custom ISR registered through the interrupt vector table.
The power story. Low-power mode was implemented across the app, with the green LaunchPad LED showing when the processor entered and exited LPM0.

2025 · Virginia Tech

Haptic Feedback
for Robotic Surgery

The idea: give surgeons back their sense of touch.

When performing delicate procedures like eye surgery, surgeons and students using forceps or other instruments lose a lot of tactile feedback. This project is an early-stage attempt to give some of that back, translating force data from the instrument into vibrational cues felt through the forearm in real time.

A load cell sensor detects force applied at the instrument. That signal runs through an HX711 amplifier to an Arduino UNO, which maps the force reading to a vibration intensity and drives a vibrotactile motor embedded in a forearm-mounted band. Press harder on the forceps, feel more vibration through the wrist.

This was also my first Arduino project. It's unfinished, genuinely pretty simple, and I'm including it because every project has a starting point. The haptic feedback work I did later in the Assistive Robotics Lab at Virginia Tech grew directly out of this.

Status: unfinished. The core loop works (force in, vibration out) but the system isn't miniaturized, isn't wireless, and hasn't been tested in a surgical context. It's a proof of concept and a learning exercise, not a finished device.
Why it matters. Vibrational intensity scaling with applied force is a simple idea, but getting it to feel intuitive is harder than it sounds. Threshold calibration, motor response curves, and latency all affect whether the feedback actually helps or just adds noise.

Left: wiring the HX711 load cell amplifier connector. The HX711 takes the tiny analog signal from the load cell and amplifies it to a range the Arduino can read digitally. This was my first time using an Arduino, and most of the learning happened here: understanding pull-up resistors, reading datasheets, getting the HX711 library talking to the sensor, and figuring out why the motor wouldn't run until the ground was properly shared.
